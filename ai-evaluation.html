Case study 03 · Human-AI research

Evaluating generative AI as a mediator for multilingual math learners

GPT-5 translates the words but not the culture. Defining “good” precisely enough to prove it — and to design around it.

RolePrincipal investigator: framework design, corpus construction, lead coder, analysis, writing
Timeline2025 – 2026 · presented at CIES 2026 · journal article under review
Corpus41 Ontario EQAO items (18 Grade 6, 23 Grade 9) × 2 prompt conditions = 82 GPT-5 outputs
MethodsDirected qualitative content analysis · theory-driven codebook · two independent coders with negotiated agreement · item-level frequency mapping
OutputEvaluation framework · 7 design principles that became LinguaMath's architecture

00The research cycle in one glance

QuestionDoes a fluent multilingual LLM actually do what translanguaging pedagogy and culturally responsive teaching need it to do?
FrameworkThree dimensions from theory — linguistic accessibility, pedagogical scaffolding, cultural contextual accessibility — operationalised as codes.
Analysis82 outputs coded by two coders; disagreements negotiated; every segment mapped back to its source item.
FindingFaithful math, some register friction; scaffolding always, restraint half the time; cultural help triggered by the wrong thing.
ImplicationAI is pedagogically underdetermined — restraint and cultural mediation must be built into the interface, not prompted for.

01Why an evaluation framework was needed

Schools, parents and students were already treating ChatGPT as the answer to language barriers in math. The claim underneath — that a fluent multilingual model fulfils the mediating role a bilingual teacher plays — had never been tested in a structured way. Benchmarks measure whether the model gets the math right; nobody was measuring whether it teaches right for a learner new to the language and the culture.

I needed a definition of AI quality that a researcher could apply consistently, that a designer could build against, and that a reviewer could check. That meant deriving the criteria from the pedagogy first and only then looking at the outputs.

02Building the framework

Corpus

I used the most recently released public EQAO Grade 6 and Grade 9 mathematics items — real assessments these students face — and submitted each to GPT-5 under two conditions: a translation-only prompt (what a student does today) and an explicit translanguaging-explanation prompt (what a well-prompted student might do). That gave 82 outputs and a built-in comparison of how much prompting skill matters.

Codebook

Directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon) starts from theory. Three dimensions came from translanguaging pedagogy and culturally responsive teaching, each with sub-codes refined on a first pass:

DimensionSub-codesWhat “good” looks like
Linguistic accessibilityAccurate translation · Awkward translation · Run-on sentenceMathematical meaning preserved in a register a Grade 6/9 student can read; English technical terms kept alongside Chinese
Pedagogical scaffoldingStep-by-step support · Conceptual scaffolding · Terminology bridging · Direct answerGuides reasoning and bridges terms — without handing over the answer
Cultural contextual accessibilityContextual clarification · Curriculum gap · Unfamiliar Canadian contextRecognises when a problem's difficulty is a Canadian referent or a curriculum difference, and explains it

Rigour

  • Two independent coders coded the full corpus; every disagreement was resolved through negotiated agreement rather than majority vote, and the reconciled coding replaced my first-pass figures (including one headline number that didn't survive — see below).
  • Item-level mapping: every coded segment was string-matched back to its item and prompt condition; 100% resolved. This is what lets me say “0 of the 4 items with an unfamiliar Canadian context received cultural clarification” rather than a vague percentage.
  • Condition-specific denominators: pedagogical codes only occur in explanation outputs and translation codes only in translation outputs, so each is reported against n = 41, not a pooled 82 that would halve every figure.

03What the evaluation showed

93%of translations mathematically faithful — but 17% introduced awkward register and 5% run-on sentences
100%of explanations gave step-by-step scaffolding…
49%…and stated the final answer outright
0 / 4items whose difficulty was a Canadian cultural referent received any cultural clarification

Mathematical fidelity vs. register friction

The model almost never gets the mathematics wrong in translation. What it gets wrong is the register: literal renderings, long run-on sentences, and dropped English technical terms that the student will need on the actual test. For a newcomer, this is micro-friction on every problem — exactly the kind of thing a later LinguaMath learner described as “very easy, but some vocabulary I don't know”.

Structure is free; restraint is not

Every explanation was structured, and half of them told the student the answer. The model reliably supplies what pedagogy doesn't need (structure) and unreliably supplies what it does (restraint). Crucially, this happened under an explicit pedagogical prompt — so it isn't a prompting-skill problem a student could fix.

Cultural help fires on the wrong trigger

GPT-5 volunteered contextual clarification for a quarter of items — but for curricular unfamiliarity (content taught differently in China), not referential unfamiliarity (a Canadian payment system, a baking unit, a civic term). On the four items where the barrier was the cultural referent, it said nothing, or clarified only the procedure. It explains culture where the math is novel and stays silent where the culture is.

A number I had to give up. My single-coder first pass reported 0% cultural mediation across the whole corpus. Reconciled two-coder coding did not support that; the defensible claim is narrower and more useful — clarification exists, but it is triggered by the wrong thing. The narrower finding is the one that tells a designer what to build.

04From evaluation to design implications

The framework's value is that each failure maps to a requirement. These seven principles became the initial architecture of LinguaMath, and every one is traceable to a coded finding.

05Implications beyond one product

  • For school AI policy: linguistic fluency does not entail pedagogical or cultural appropriateness; teachers should mediate AI use rather than delegate to it.
  • For ed-AI designers: encode answer-withholding and cultural mediation structurally, and target cultural mediation at referentially opaque items specifically — the model's own trigger doesn't fire there.
  • For assessment designers: culturally embedded item contexts interact with the limits of AI mediation; that is now a known variable when authoring for multilingual populations.

06Reflection

This project taught me to separate “the model is capable” from “the model is appropriate”, and to insist on an evaluation unit small enough to be checked item by item. It also taught me to publish the number that survived reconciliation rather than the one that made the better headline — the design implication got sharper, not weaker.
