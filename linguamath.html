Case study 01 · Flagship

LinguaMath: designing and evaluating AI-mediated multilingual math support

A newcomer student stopped using the prototype for ten days. Finding out why changed the product's architecture.

RoleLead researcher, designer & developer (solo build; supervised doctoral research)
TimelineOct 2025 – present · 5 iterations across 2 rounds
Participants1 translanguaging researcher, 1 math teacher, 5 Grade 6–7 newcomer learners (Round 1); live cohort of ~23 students (pilot deployment)
MethodsStakeholder interviews · usability sessions · longitudinal follow-up · inductive thematic analysis · event instrumentation · LLM-as-judge audit
OutputLive app at linguamath.ca · long paper under review · AERA 2027 submission

00The research cycle in one glance

ProblemNewcomer students weren't engaging consistently with an English-only AI math interface — and unstructured AI gave away answers.
ResearchStakeholder consultations, two-session usability testing with a longitudinal gap, then a four-learner pilot; all sessions in Mandarin.
FindingBilingual UI wasn't a preference — it was an access precondition, upstream of onboarding. Cultural “anchoring” was contested.
DesignBilingual UI by default, first-login tour, structured bilingual AI output, curriculum-tagged hints that never reveal answers.
ValidationSecond cohort independently re-asked for the same things and validated the no-direct-answer hint unprompted.

01Problem

Chinese newcomer students in Ontario arrive with strong mathematics — the Chinese curriculum typically covers Ontario Grade 7 content by Grade 4–6 — yet they underperform on English-language assessments such as EQAO. The barrier is linguistic and cultural access, not mathematical knowledge. Students were already reaching for ChatGPT and translators to cope, but my earlier evaluation of raw GPT-5 output (case study 03) showed that unstructured AI hands over the answer half the time, over-scaffolds, and never explains the Canadian cultural context that actually trips these learners up.

The design question: what does an AI math helper have to do differently for a learner who is new to the language, new to the curriculum, and new to the country?

02Research design

I ran this as design-based research (DBR): each iteration produced a working build, evidence from real users, and a documented decision. Round 1 had two iterations; Round 2 has three more (in progress). Every session was audio-recorded, transcribed with speaker turns, and coded inductively (Braun & Clarke). Sessions were conducted in Mandarin with English code-switching for mathematical terms; I coded the Mandarin directly.

Ethics: REB-approved; informed consent, assent for minors, parental consent; pseudonyms throughout; instrumentation captures application events only — no audio, video, screen recording or runtime PII.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — Bilingual UI is an access precondition, not a preference

Between her two sessions, Student R did not open the prototype once. Her explanation was unambiguous:

“我看不懂阿里是什么” — I can't read what the interface says. Student R, Nov 10 follow-up

A tutorial I had added between sessions — itself in English — did not help. This reframed the problem: a learner who cannot read the interface cannot discover the language toggle that would make it readable. In Round 2, all four learners independently raised the same requirement; the twins found the toggle and switched to Chinese on first use, Student N asked for “中英互译” outright. Bilingual UI had to be the default, upstream of onboarding.

Finding 2 — “Never give the answer” was validated by the strongest students, unprompted

The hint mode is hard-constrained to at most two guiding questions per turn and can never state the final answer. I expected pushback; instead the highest-performing learners endorsed it without being asked, and the teacher independently endorsed the constraint (the researcher's caution: don't over-compress). This confirmed a principle carried over from the GPT-5 evaluation: restraint has to be encoded in the system, not requested by the user.

Finding 3 — Cultural anchoring at the referent level was contested

The original “Relate to Your Culture” mode tried to map every problem to a Chinese analogue. Three of four confident learners rejected it (“没什么不好理解的吧” — what's hard to understand here?), the teacher found the label “oriented”, and the researcher called the outputs “尬” — forced, sometimes fabricating parallels. Yet Round 2 teacher data showed word-problem framing (“a beaker”, Canadian payment systems) is culturally loaded. The resolution was to move anchoring from referents to register: explain the Canadian context first, bridge only when there's a real asymmetry, and say openly when there is no cultural barrier.

Finding 4 — Three stakeholders converged on the same folder model from different cultural references

The teacher proposed Google-Drive-style folders; Student R proposed the Bilibili 收藏夹 model; the researcher validated the Chinese 错题本 (mistake-notebook) tradition as “a very important concept in China”. Three independent people, two cultural models, one design. That convergence was the strongest single signal in Round 1.

Generative hypothesis. All four learners rated Ontario math as easy (“太顺畅了” — too smooth) while their population underperforms on EQAO. I read this as an access gap, not a knowledge gap — which reframed LinguaMath from a remediation tool into an access intervention and became the argument of the AERA 2027 paper.

Architecture that followed from the research

  • Curriculum RAG — Ontario Grade 6–8 expectations (168 specific expectations as JSON), 623-term glossary, the Chinese curriculum (OCR'd), and automatic grade detection, so every response is anchored to a specific expectation rather than generic “math help”.
  • Hint mode constraints — ≤ 2 guiding questions per turn, no final answer; enforced at the system-prompt level and audited asynchronously by an LLM-as-judge that flags direct-answer leakage.
  • Cultural anchor library — strand-organised Chinese referents plus explicit anti-patterns (don't anchor trivial nouns; never fabricate; name “no barrier” as a valid output).
  • Two-layer register-first ladder (Round 2) — inline hover glosses on technical nouns/verbs so students can do the math first; Question Analysis and Hint only if they get it wrong.

05Validation & impact

4/4Round 2 learners independently re-asked for bilingual UI — the redesign's core premise held
3independent stakeholders converged on the folder model that shipped
23instrumented interaction events now live in production for the quantitative phase
2papers under review + 1 AERA submission built on this audit trail

Round 2 (May–June 2026) confirmed the direction and sharpened it: a translanguaging researcher and the students agreed that technical nouns and verbs need glossing while referent words don't, and the teacher's counter-evidence about culturally loaded problem framing produced the two-layer delivery rather than a winner-take-all decision. The live deployment is now collecting interaction data for a separate quantitative phase — efficacy claims are explicitly scoped out until then.

06What I'd do differently

  • Ship the tutorial bilingual on day one. I fixed the access barrier and then reintroduced it in the onboarding. It's the clearest lesson in the project: every layer a newcomer touches first has to be in their language.
  • Recruit for disconfirmation. Families willing to pilot an AI math tool are confident and resourced. I now report the confidence–EQAO gap as a hypothesis, not a finding, and I'm recruiting less-confident learners in Round 2.
  • Verify ASR transcripts against audio before quoting. Automatic transcription garbles technical Mandarin (名词 → 名次). The Round 2 papers paraphrase until every quote is checked.
